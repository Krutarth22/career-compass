# Design for Manufacturing: Prototype to Production Part

## Production Workflow Mirrored
1. Prototyping quickly to validate function
2. Selecting the production process and material from volume and cost
3. Redesigning for that process (draft, wall thickness, tool access)
4. Getting quotes and design-for-manufacturing feedback from suppliers
5. Releasing a production-ready part with cost and lead-time data

## What You'll Build
One part from your assembly taken from a 3D-printed prototype to a
production-ready design for a chosen process (injection molding, CNC
machining, sheet metal, or die casting): a process and material
selection with rationale at three volumes, a redesign that follows the
process's design rules, quotes or DFM feedback from at least two
suppliers or online manufacturing services, a cost breakdown, and a
released drawing with process-specific notes.

## Student-Scope Notes
- Online manufacturing services provide instant quotes and DFM
  feedback for free; use them as your "suppliers."
- Actually ordering the part is optional but recommended if affordable;
  the redesign and quotes are the core.
- Choose a process that suits the part; justify it against the
  alternatives.

## Steps
1. Print the prototype, test its function, and log what the prototype
   revealed about the design.
2. Estimate demand at three volumes (10, 1,000, 100,000) and compare
   candidate processes and materials on cost, lead time, and properties.
3. Choose the process and material and write down its design rules
   (draft angles, minimum wall, radii, tolerances, tool access).
4. Redesign the part for the process, checking every rule, and run the
   CAD tool's manufacturability analysis if available.
5. Upload to at least two manufacturing services, collect quotes at the
   three volumes and any DFM warnings, and revise to clear the warnings.
6. Build the cost breakdown: tooling, per-part price by volume, and the
   break-even between processes.
7. Update the drawing with process notes (parting line, gate location,
   grain direction, bend radii) and the material specification.
8. Write up the journey from prototype to production design with the
   quotes, the redesign decisions, and the cost model.

## Extension Ideas
- Design the injection mold core and cavity for the part.
- Run a moldflow or similar simulation.
- Order the production part and compare to the prototype.
- Redesign the same part for a second process and compare cost.

## Skills Demonstrated
- Prototype validation and process selection
- Design for manufacturing rule application
- Supplier quoting and DFM feedback loops
- Cost modeling across volumes

## Industry Relevance

Consumer Products, Medical Devices, Automotive Suppliers. Mechanical engineers in these sectors are valued for designing parts that can be made at cost, and the shift from prototype to production is where inexperienced designers stumble. A part with supplier DFM feedback and a volume cost model demonstrates production readiness.
